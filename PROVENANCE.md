# Provenance and Archival History

## Purpose

This document records the origin, custody, transformation, organization, and preservation history of the materials in this repository. Its purpose is to help future readers distinguish original historical evidence from later digitization, transcription, editing, and repository activity.

## Creator and collecting context

The corpus was created and assembled by **Djuro Zivkovic** during his compositional and intellectual work over several decades.

The materials document a continuing creative process and may include:

- compositional ideas and sketches;
- musical observations and theoretical reflections;
- philosophical and religious writing;
- personal reflections and diary-like entries;
- essays and fragments;
- plans, lists, annotations, and marginalia;
- connections between written thought, musical works, performances, and publications.

The corpus is a personal and compositional archive rather than a set of documents originally created according to a single filing system.

## Original formats

Source materials may originate as:

- bound paper notebooks;
- loose handwritten or printed sheets;
- annotations in books, scores, or other documents;
- word-processing documents;
- plain-text or Markdown files;
- notation files, including MusicXML or LilyPond sources;
- email, notes-app, or database exports;
- photographs, scans, audio, or audiovisual records;
- files recovered from earlier computers, storage devices, or backup media.

The current digital form may therefore differ from the form in which an item was first created.

## Custodial history

Unless a more specific record states otherwise, the materials remained in the custody of Djuro Zivkovic before being organized and added to this repository.

When an item has passed through another person, institution, computer system, publication, or storage location, that history should be recorded in the item's metadata. Unknown stages of custody must be marked as unknown rather than reconstructed without evidence.

## Ingestion into the archive

Material may be added individually or as part of an accession batch. A batch can represent, for example:

- one physical notebook;
- one folder of loose papers;
- one export from a notes application;
- one directory from an earlier computer;
- one recovered storage device;
- one group of newly created notes.

The date of ingestion or Git commit is not automatically the original creation date.

Where practical, each accession record should preserve:

- accession identifier;
- date received or processed;
- source and previous location;
- original filenames and directory paths;
- physical container or carrier;
- responsible person;
- actions performed during ingestion;
- known omissions, damage, or uncertainties.

## Arrangement and identifiers

Materials may be arranged in broad working collections such as `NOTAE`, `SCRIPTA`, or other categories. These categories are interpretive and may change as the archive develops.

Each independently catalogued item receives a permanent alphanumeric identifier. The identifier does not encode folder location, chronology, subject, genre, or intellectual relationships.

Branches, continuations, revisions, translations, and other connections are expressed through metadata relationships rather than through increasingly complex hierarchical identifiers.

Useful relationships include:

- `part-of`
- `continues`
- `developed-from`
- `revision-of`
- `translation-of`
- `transcription-of`
- `excerpt-from`
- `related-to`
- `supersedes`

Identifiers are never reused. If an identifier is assigned in error, merged, withdrawn, or found to duplicate another item, its status and replacement relationship should remain recorded.

## Dating policy

Historical dates are recorded according to the available evidence.

Recommended values include:

- `exact`
- `approximate`
- `inferred`
- `unknown`

When a date is inferred, the evidence should be stated, for example references to a known composition, performance, publication, place, person, paper type, file-system timestamp, or adjacent dated entry.

The following dates must not be conflated:

- original date of creation;
- date of later revision;
- date of digitization;
- date of transcription;
- date of cataloguing;
- date added to Git;
- date of archival release.

File-system timestamps are treated as supporting evidence only, since they can change during copying, conversion, recovery, or migration.

## Digitization

Digitized images should preserve the informational content and physical context of the source. Where applicable, digitization should include covers, bindings, blank pages, inserts, page edges, and other features relevant to interpretation.

For each digitized object, metadata should record, when known:

- source item identifier;
- digitization date;
- operator;
- equipment and software;
- resolution and colour mode;
- file format;
- processing performed;
- missing, illegible, damaged, or obscured areas.

Preservation masters should remain separate from access derivatives. Routine enhancement, cropping, compression, optical character recognition, or conversion must not silently replace the master capture.

## Transcription and editorial intervention

A transcription is a later representation of the source, not the source itself.

Transcriptions should preserve meaningful spelling, punctuation, capitalization, lineation, deletions, insertions, and uncertainty when relevant to interpretation. Editorial normalization should be declared rather than silently introduced.

Illegible, uncertain, supplied, or editorially reconstructed text should be marked consistently. Each transcription should identify its source item and, where practical, the person and date responsible for the transcription.

Translations, edited reading versions, and substantially revised texts should be clearly distinguished from diplomatic transcriptions.

## Born-digital material

For born-digital items, the original received file should be retained whenever possible. Normalized or open-format derivatives may be created for preservation and access, but they should not overwrite the source file.

Relevant provenance may include:

- original filename and path;
- application and version, when known;
- operating system, when relevant;
- file creation and modification timestamps;
- export or conversion history;
- checksums taken at ingestion;
- known corruption, recovery, or repair.

## Repository history

Git records the history of files after they enter the repository. It does not necessarily record the earlier history of the underlying text or idea.

Repository commits may represent:

- initial ingestion;
- transcription;
- correction;
- metadata enrichment;
- reclassification;
- privacy review;
- format migration;
- preparation of a public release.

Commit history should not be used alone to infer the date of original authorship.

## Integrity and authentication

Complete archival releases should include a SHA-256 checksum manifest. When cryptographic signing is implemented, the release tag or checksum manifest may also carry a detached OpenPGP signature.

Checksums demonstrate whether files have changed. Cryptographic signatures can associate a particular release with a signing key. Neither mechanism, by itself, replaces documentary provenance, creator identification, custody records, or the original physical and digital sources.

The public key fingerprint used for official releases should be published in `AUTHORSHIP.md` and through independent identity-bearing sources.

## Privacy, restrictions, and rights

The corpus may contain private information, religious or personal reflections, and information concerning living or identifiable people. Preservation does not automatically imply immediate public access.

Each item may be assigned an access status such as:

- open;
- restricted until a stated date or condition;
- available to approved researchers;
- closed during the lifetime of named persons;
- closed pending rights or privacy review.

Copyright, privacy, confidentiality, ownership of physical objects, and permission for public access are separate questions and should be documented separately.

## Preservation history

Every major preservation action should be logged, including:

- copying to new media;
- checksum verification;
- recovery from damaged media;
- format conversion or migration;
- replacement of corrupted files;
- creation of public derivatives;
- deposit with an external repository or institution;
- changes to access restrictions.

The log should state the date, responsible person or institution, affected files, software or method, and outcome.

## Known limitations

The archive is expected to contain incomplete, undated, duplicated, fragmentary, and evolving material. Some historical relationships or dates may never be recoverable with certainty.

Absence from this repository does not demonstrate that an item never existed. Classification in a folder does not establish an exclusive genre or subject. A transcription or digital derivative must not be assumed to reproduce every physical feature of its source.

Corrections to provenance should be documented through version history rather than silently erasing earlier archival descriptions.
